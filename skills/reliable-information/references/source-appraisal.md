# Source appraisal decisions

## Fit, independence, and accessibility

Write the claim in one sentence and identify the evidence needed to support it. A standard can
establish an interface requirement. A controlled comparison can estimate an effect under studied
conditions. A professional book can explain a decision framework. These are different evidentiary
roles. Do not use an impressive source to support a claim it never evaluated.

Identify the original evidentiary origin. Ten summaries of one survey are not ten replications.
Separate reporting, the quoted institution's statement, and the underlying document. Inspect actual
disclosures, corrections, publication status, and the author's relevant expertise. An accredited
qualification does not make every statement well supported. A commercial affiliation is a potential
conflict to examine, not automatic proof of falsehood.

## Scientific material

Confirm design and applicability. Check the question, population, comparator, intervention/exposure,
outcome definitions, sampling, uncertainty, confounders, missing data, and limitations. Distinguish
preregistered analyses from exploratory findings where that distinction affects interpretation.
Read corrections and linked notices rather than silently citing the original version. For a review,
look at how evidence was selected and assessed. Do not treat the word systematic as sufficient.

## Traceability and stopping

Keep concise notes tying consequential claims to inspected passages, versions, and dates. Do not
collect private source copies or create an unsolicited research database. If access is limited,
state the limitation and look for lawful alternative versions. Stop with an explicit uncertainty
when further reliable evidence is unavailable. Never fill the gap with invented confidence.
